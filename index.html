Where Humanity, Technology & the Environment meet, and collide.

Crossroads gathers essays, short stories, and multimedia work from students, faculty, and neighbors across the Great Lakes region to ask one question together: how might we reimagine our relationships with each other, with technology, and with the Earth?

Why a crossroads?

Three domains we usually study apart

Most conversations treat people, tools, and the planet as separate subjects, or at best, pairs. The most pressing questions of our time live where all three overlap.

Humanity

Who lives here?

How we earn a living, how we connect, who gets a say, and whose lived experience shapes the decisions made about a place.

Technology

What are we building?

The tools we rely on every day, and the physical infrastructure, power, and labor hidden behind every search, stream, and chatbot.

Environment

What does it cost the Earth?

The water drawn, the power consumed, the land reshaped, and what we leave behind for the ecosystems and generations that come after us.

Rooted in the Great Lakes

A region shaped by water

Crossroads is based in Cleveland, on the shore of Lake Erie, and focused on the Great Lakes region. Eight U.S. states, the province of Ontario, and many Tribal Nations and First Nations share this basin, which holds about a fifth of the world's surface fresh water.

That water, and the land around it, is exactly what makes the region attractive to data center developers. It's also why the people who live here deserve a say in what gets built.

We welcome voices from all around the lakes, from Duluth to Buffalo and from Toronto to Toledo.

Our Story

Crossroads Journal began as a capstone project to explore the places where Humanity, Technology, and the Environment meet, and, more specifically, where they collide.

We chose to launch with a focus on data centers because few subjects make that intersection, and that collision, more visible. From the water they draw to the power they consume to the land they reshape, data centers influence and change the communities living in their shadow. Decisions about how we build and operate data centers are rarely made in public view, and even more rarely shaped by the local people most affected by them.

Crossroads Journal exists to open that conversation to more voices through essays, short stories, and multimedia work from contributors both inside and beyond Case Western Reserve University, with a focus on the Great Lakes region. Our journal is guided by a rotating editorial board of students and faculty who select work consistent with the journal's mission.

While we're launching with data centers as our starting point, our ambition is broader. We aim to grow over time into a home for creative work across the full intersection of Humanity, Technology, and the Environment.

Crossroads is a project of the Humanity and Technology (HAT) Capstone at Case Western Reserve University, Cleveland, Ohio.

Mission Statement

At Crossroads Journal we aim to elevate diverse perspectives on problems that arise at the intersection of humanity, technology, and the environment. Through creative works that range from essays to short stories and multimedia pieces, we encourage our contributors and readers to reimagine ways in which we can make change at this intersection: change that benefits all communities equitably.

What we believe

Lived experience is expertise

The people closest to a problem often understand it best, and are least often asked.

Creativity is a tool for change

A story or photograph can move people in ways a policy brief can't.

Decisions should be made in the open

We push back on choices shaped behind closed doors, by institutional bias, or by colonial ways of thinking about land and resources.

Many voices, not one answer

We publish disagreement. A crossroads is a place where different paths meet.

Terms of Publication

Your work stays yours

By accepting publication, the author grants Crossroads Journal first publication rights, on a non-exclusive basis, to the work for our website, print and anthology editions, and promotional materials, including the right to keep the piece archived on our site indefinitely and to include it in future anthologized editions of the journal.

The author retains copyright and is free to publish the piece elsewhere after it first appears in Crossroads Journal. If you republish it elsewhere, we'd appreciate a "first published in Crossroads Journal" credit, the same courtesy we extend when reprinting work that first appeared elsewhere.

At this time, we do not offer compensation for acceptance or publication.

This Year's Theme · Volume 1 · 2026–2027

The Cloud Has a Footprint

"The cloud" sounds weightless. It isn't. It's concrete, cooling water, transmission lines, and diesel generators, built on land someone calls home. Our first volume is rooted in advocacy around data centers in the Great Lakes region, and in the people and places that live alongside them.

Why data centers

The intersection, made visible

Few things show the collision of Humanity, Technology, and the Environment as clearly as the data center. Every search, stream, and AI prompt runs through one. Their growth raises questions about how we live, what we consume, what we leave behind, and how we connect with the world around us.

They also raise questions of power: who decides where they go, who benefits, and who bears the cost. Too often, these decisions are made through non-disclosure agreements and closed-door negotiations, rarely shaped by the local people most affected.

Advocacy doesn't mean every piece must agree. It means insisting that the people living with these decisions get to be heard.

We welcome work from every angle: residents, workers, engineers, skeptics, and enthusiasts.

Close to home

Northeast Ohio is already part of this story

Cleveland sits on Lake Erie, part of the largest surface freshwater system on Earth. That makes the Great Lakes region attractive to data center developers, and it makes the questions of water, power, and oversight here urgent. Lake Erie's watershed is already stressed by nutrient pollution and harmful algal blooms.

In 2026, Clevelanders and Ohioans made their voices heard. A hyperscale data center permit was rejected in Slavic Village, Cleveland City Council approved a short-term moratorium on new data center projects, and after more than 7,000 public comments, Ohio EPA withdrew a proposed fast-track wastewater permit for data centers.

Prompts to get you started

Questions we're sitting with

Not requirements, just doors in. Answer one, argue with one, or ignore them all.

  1. What does the cloud look like from your street, your kitchen tap, or your electric bill?
  2. Who was in the room when the decision was made, and who wasn't?
  3. What would a data center designed with its neighbors look like?
  4. Write a letter to Lake Erie, a server, or your future self in 2050.
  5. What do we owe the land that holds our memories, both digital and human?
  6. Imagine the data center's afterlife: what happens to the building, and the place, when it's gone?

Our commitments

A safe space to share

We're compiling and distributing other people's creative work, and we take that responsibility seriously.

  • We never alter the meaning of your work, and every edit is made with you.
  • Your work is credited to you, and you keep your copyright.
  • We don't use contributors' work for anyone's personal gain.
  • Editorial decisions are made as a group, guided by our mission.
  • We actively seek out perspectives that usually go unheard.
